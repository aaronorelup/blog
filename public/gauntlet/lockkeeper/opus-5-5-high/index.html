A lock is a lift for boats

A canal cannot run uphill, so it is cut into level pools joined by locks. A lock is a chamber with gates at both ends. To go up, a boat enters the chamber while it is low, the gates close, and the keeper lets water in from the pool above until the chamber is level with it. To go down, the chamber is emptied into the pool below.

Every lockage costs water

Filling a chamber takes its volume out of the pool above; emptying it pours that water into the pool below. So water only ever runs downhill. The only new water is a feeder stream into the summit pool. A pool may never drop below its minimum, so sometimes a boat must wait until water arrives from higher up. Pools that are already full spill over a weir into the pool below.

The chamber must be the right way round

A boat going up needs the chamber low. If the last boat went up, the chamber is full, and someone has to empty it first: an empty turn. Boats going the opposite way share the work nicely; boats going the same way pay for a turn each time. Two narrowboats (or one barge) fit in a chamber together.

Keepers do the work

Nothing moves without a keeper at the lock for the whole lockage, and there are only a few keepers for many locks, so they walk the towpath between jobs. The canal is open 06:00 to 22:00 and some locks close for maintenance.

The score

Each boat has a deadline and a priority (1 to 3). Weighted lateness is the sum of priority × minutes late: lower is better. Ties are broken by water drawn (total volume taken by up-lockages).
