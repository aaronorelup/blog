Boats climb this hillside canal through locks — water lifts with gates at both ends — and three keepers must be at a lock for every operation. Press Play to watch the plan run.
A lock is a water lift for boats. The canal climbs a hill in steps. Between two stretches of water (pools) sits a chamber with gates at both ends. To go up: enter the chamber at the low level, close the gate, let water in from the pool above until the chamber is level with it, then sail out. Going down is the same in reverse. Nine locks, ten pools.
Only three keepers work them. Every fill or empty needs a keeper standing at that lock the whole time, and keepers walk between locks (the little figures on the bank). The chamber must be in the right state for each boat, so after an up-trip it often has to be emptied again with no boat in it (an empty turn).
Water is limited. Every fill draws water from the pool above; a pool may never fall below its minimum. Locks only work 06:00–22:00. Each boat has a deadline; the plan is scored by lateness × priority. Click a boat to follow it; drag the time slider to scrub.

    How a canal lock works

    The rules of this canal, briefly

    • A boat going up passes locks from+1 … to, each with an up operation (chamber fills from the pool above). Going down: down operations (chamber empties into the pool below).
    • A chamber holds two slots: two narrowboats or one barge. All boats in one operation go the same way.
    • An operation takes fill/empty time, plus time to enter and leave if any boat is aboard. It must sit inside opening hours and clear of maintenance closures.
    • A keeper must stand at the lock for the whole operation; walking takes time.
    • Each fill takes water out of the pool above at its start; a pool may never fall below its minimum. A feeder stream tops up the summit.

    How the plan is scored

    Lateness of a boat = minutes after its deadline when it leaves its last lock. Weighted lateness = ∑ priority × lateness (priority 3 is the doctor’s launch, 1 a holiday boat). Lower is better; ties are broken by total water drawn. An invalid schedule scores nothing, so the solver and the page both re-check every rule.

    Where the numbers come from

    Scores, water levels, waiting reasons and the disruption re-plans are all computed in this page by the same engine (solve.js) that wrote the schedule — nothing about the delays is typed in by hand.
